# Squamata_Mitonuclear_Coevolution

Scripts and files repository for "*A Network-based Perspective of Mitonuclear Coevolution in Squamata*", authored by Oscar Wallnoefer, Mirko Martini, Giorgia Galletti, Niccolò Gabriele, Marco Passamonti, Federico Plazzi.

Affiliation: `University of Bologna`

Supplementary Data: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31455355

### Abstract

**Background**
The mitochondrial and nuclear genomes must coevolve to ensure cell homeostasis, legacy and consequence of their endosymbiotic origin. Mitonuclear coevolution is central to the oxidative phosphorylation pathway (OXPHOS), where proteins encoded in both genomes interact. However, mitochondrial products are involved in many other pathways, and the extent of mitonuclear coevolution beyond OXPHOS remains largely uncharacterized. This goal could be achieved through an extensive use of evolutionary rate covariation (ERC), able to detect coevolutionary relationships by identifying gene pairs whose evolutionary rates covary across a phylogeny. We used the Squamata order as a model system, since this clade shows a deep phylogenetic discordance between nuclear and mitochondrial markers, allowing the ERC results to be validated against other phylogenetic methods.

**Results** 
We found a robust ERC signal within OXPHOS genes in Squamata: nuclear-encoded subunits covary with the mitochondrial ones, in particular those at the physical mitonuclear interface. Then, we scaled up ERC to nearly three million protein pairs across 34 squamate proteomes, reconstructing a coevolutionary network. Mitochondrial OXPHOS genes emerge as network hubs, with coevolutionary neighborhoods encompassing broader energetic pathways, alongside a signal for neuron development. In parallel, mitonuclear coevolution left a marked phylogenetic signature: nuclear OXPHOS subunits that physically interact with mitochondrial subunits mirrored the mitochondrial topology, unlike non-contact ones. Only a few other mitochondrion-associated genes showed the same pattern.

**Conclusions**
ERC finely captures clues of coevolution, producing a graded signal across the proteome. By its nature, this method cannot disentangle the relative contribution of structural compensation and subtler functional dependencies. High ERC values are therefore not necessarily linked to balancing mutations at the interacting proteins’ interface, but rather reveal which nuclear pathways share a similar evolutionary trajectory with mitochondrial genes. Phylogenetic analyses also provide support for mitonuclear coevolution, but topological mirroring likely requires a stronger signal than ERC alone captures.
